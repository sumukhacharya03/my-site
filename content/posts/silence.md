I woke up on the third morning feeling just... off.

Not sick. Not sad. Just groggy, restless, and quietly irritated for no reason I could explain. And then it hit me — I had deleted Instagram two days ago.

It started with a number.

Three to four hours. Every single day. That's what my screen time report said, and honestly, seeing it written out like that shook me a little. But it wasn't just the time that bothered me. It was what Instagram was doing to me underneath all of it.

Every scroll was a dopamine hit. Every reel pulled me somewhere new before I'd even processed the last one. Slowly, without realising it, I was becoming more reactive, more restless, more emotionally drained — and I didn't like the person I was turning into.

So I deleted it.

The first two days felt great. Lighter. Calmer. Like I'd put down something heavy I didn't know I was carrying.

Then came the third morning.

Standing in that weird fog, I realised something — just deleting an app doesn't fix anything. The hours were still there, empty and waiting. And empty time doesn't stay empty. It just fills with a different kind of noise.

I didn't need less. I needed to replace the mindless with something meaningful.

So I sat down with a piece of paper and started writing. No phone, no screen. Just an honest list of what I wanted to let go of, and what I actually wanted to make space for.

That piece of paper is what became this blog.

*Silence is Loud* isn't really about quitting social media. It's about what you start to hear when you finally go quiet.

### What I'm Cutting Out — Until the End of 2026

These aren't impulsive decisions. Each one is something I noticed was quietly draining me, and I'm done pretending otherwise.

1. **Instagram** — Deleted. The dopamine loop was real and it had to go.

2. **Music** — No more using it as a constant background escape. Silence is the point.

3. **Games** — Easy time sink, zero return. Gone.

4. **WhatsApp & Phone** — No more always-on notifications. Two time blocks only: 8 AM (10 mins) and 9 PM (20 mins). Outside of that, the timer is set to zero and the phone stays far away.

5. **Sleeping and Waking Up Late** — In bed by 10–11 PM, up by 5–6 AM. Non-negotiable.

6. **YouTube** — Blocked by default. If something genuinely needs it, I unblock, use it in Unhook mode, and that's it.

7. **Headphones & Earphones** — Only for office calls or when I need to not disturb someone. Not for zoning out.

8. **TV Shows & Movies** — Replacing mindless watching with books, long podcasts, and long-form YouTube that actually adds something. That said, on days where I'm genuinely drained — a funny video or a light watch is totally fair. Rest is part of the process too.

That's the list. Eight things. Some feel easy, some already feel uncomfortable — which is probably a sign they needed to go.

### What I'm Building Instead

Cutting things out only works if you're intentional about what fills the space. So here's what I'm replacing it all with —

1. **Journaling** — 5 minutes every morning and 5 minutes every night. Just me, a pen, and whatever's on my mind.

2. **DSA Practice** — 1.5 to 2 hours every day. This is the work that actually moves the needle for me.

3. **Reading** — At least 1 hour a day. Books, articles, research papers — anything that makes me think.

4. **Movement** — 45 minutes minimum, every single day. The body needs it as much as the mind does.

### Day 0 — Baseline

Before this journey means anything, I needed to know where I was actually starting from. Not a gut feeling, not a rough guess — real numbers. So on Day 0, April 3rd, I sat down and took two sets of tests: a Human Benchmark cognitive test and a Mental Health self-assessment. I'll retake these at the end to see whether any of this actually made a difference.

#### What the Numbers Say

On the cognitive side, the scores aren't catastrophic — but they're not great either. Reaction time, memory, focus — all areas that should improve as the noise clears.

The mental health side is harder to look at. Well-being sitting at 24/100. Anxiety at 19/21. Depression at 23/27. Stress at 26/40. These aren't comfortable numbers to share, but that's exactly why I'm sharing them. This is the baseline. This is what unchecked overstimulation quietly does to you over time.

#### Taking the Tests

Taking these tests was revealing in a way I didn't expect. Somewhere halfway through, I noticed something unsettling — I was restless. Legs shaking, frustration building, a quiet agitation that had no real source. My brain was actively resisting the act of sitting still and focusing. Between levels, I'd feel this impatient pull, like the pause itself was unbearable.

The number memory test hit hardest — I'd hold the number, feel confident, and then watch it dissolve into fog before I could get it down. Not because it was too long. Just because my brain had already moved on before I gave it permission to.

That scared me more than the scores did.

### My Hypothesis

I am treating this strictly as an experiment, and every experiment needs a hypothesis.

By removing the constant dopamine hits — the reels, the music, the games, the endless scrolling — I believe my baseline tolerance for boredom will increase. And when the brain is bored enough, it stops waiting for entertainment to arrive. It starts making its own. That is when it reaches for coding, reading, writing, mathematics — things that are harder, slower, and infinitely more rewarding.

I also believe that silence is necessary for original thought. Every waking hour filled with noise is an hour the brain never gets to process what it has already taken in. Strip that away, and something shifts. Thoughts connect differently. Ideas that were buried under the noise start surfacing on their own.

The first 2–3 weeks will likely be brutal — withdrawal, restlessness, and phantom music playing in my head even when the room is completely silent. That last part is real, and I am genuinely curious to see how long it lasts.

But by Month 3, my hypothesis is this: clearer thinking, sharper focus, and a mind that no longer needs external noise to function. A brain that has learned to be comfortable in its own company.

### The Rules

Every experiment needs guardrails. Here are mine.

**If I slip —** It happens. If I reinstall an app or fall back into old habits, I won't spiral about it. I'll open my [Dopamine Detox Sheet](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1fD-VhNiD2euw8HSTs9bIQlsLOI7niGa6EgfD7Q6QBKk/edit?usp=sharing) and log it — the date, the trigger, what I consumed, how long it lasted, how I felt after, and what I'll do differently. Then I move on.

**If negative thoughts show up —** I'll face them instead of drowning them out with music like I used to. I'll observe them, let them pass, and journal it out. But if things get genuinely heavy, I'll pull myself out — a walk, a hobby, something light to watch — and then write it down. No bottling things up.

**If I hear music accidentally —** A mall, a café, a friend's place — I won't run away. I just won't focus on it. The goal is to remove the *choice* to listen, not to fear the environment.

The goal was never perfection. It's to keep going.
