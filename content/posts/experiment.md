## Part 1 — The Breaking Point

It was a Monday evening, and I had done nothing I planned to do that day.

My manager was on leave, which as an intern basically means free will — no deadlines breathing down your neck, no one checking in. He had asked me to go through the entire codebase of a new project I'd been assigned to. Simple enough. But I didn't do it. My phone was in my hand the entire day, and I kept toggling between games, reels, music, and YouTube in this cycle that was so addictive that even when I didn't want to be doing it, it just... happened. Over and over, until I looked up and the day was gone.

And through all of it — headphones in. Always. Music bleeding into reels bleeding into YouTube, one constant stream of noise pumped directly into my ears for hours on end. I'd stopped noticing it. That's the thing about headphones when they become a habit — they stop being something you choose and start being something you just always have on.

That moment — that specific Monday evening — made me feel like I had no interest in life.

I started replaying things in my head. Not hours, but *days* — calculating how many actual days I had lost to this cycle. How much potential I was quietly burning through. How this was changing me as a person in ways I didn't like. There's a saying I kept coming back to: *"You are not a man until you overcome your urges."* I'm not claiming that's the complete definition of becoming someone, but in that moment it cut deep, because I felt exactly like someone who had no control over himself.

And then I thought about my dad. He has always been there — supported me, never once made me feel unworthy. The question that hit me was simple and brutal: what would he think if he could see exactly how I'd spent that day?

So I deleted everything. All of it. Right then.

And then I reinstalled it all the next day, slowly, like poison.

That's when I knew the problem wasn't the apps. It was that I had no plan, no structure, nothing to replace the void with. Deleting apps without a system is just white-knuckling it — and willpower alone has never beaten a dopamine loop. So I deleted everything again, but this time I sat down and built something: a streak tracker in Google Sheets, with every date as a row and every bad habit as a column. Green means I didn't touch it. Dark red means I did. I started it that same day, even though I knew every cell would be red — because if I waited for a "good day" to start, I'd be waiting forever.

And that was the moment this stopped being just another failed attempt at self-control, and started becoming something bigger. This experiment isn't just about reclaiming focus or being more productive. It's about seeing what my brain actually looks like without the noise — including giving my ears a real chance to recover from hours of daily punishment they never asked for.

## Part 2 — The Protocol

Deleting apps and reinstalling them the next day taught me one thing: decision fatigue is real, and willpower is not a system. So this time I didn't rely on motivation. I built a protocol.

### What I Removed

The first cut was the obvious stuff. Instagram — gone. Every game on my phone — gone. Every music streaming app — gone. YouTube on my phone — disabled. On my laptop, blocked entirely, with one exception: if I genuinely need it, I unblock it for that session only and use it in Unhook mode, which strips out recommendations, shorts, and the homepage. Just the video. Nothing else pulling you in.

### What I Restructured

WhatsApp was trickier. Deleting it wasn't realistic — it's how I communicate with family, friends, my workplace. So instead I set the daily timer to zero minutes and locked it behind four fixed windows. I can open it at 8 am for 5 minutes, 1 pm for 10 minutes, 5 pm for 5 minutes, and 9 pm for 10 minutes. That's 30 minutes of WhatsApp a day. The rest of the time, the phone itself is on DND and physically kept away. The phone blocks and WhatsApp windows are the same time slots — so it's not just the app, it's the whole device.

The phone is also set to full grayscale. Sounds small. It isn't — colour is a core part of how these apps are designed to hold your attention. Grey is boring. Boring is the point.

Headphones stay off unless I'm in an office meeting or in a situation where I'd genuinely disturb someone around me. No more music as a constant background escape.

### What I Added

This part matters as much as what I removed — because the void left by all that stimulation needs to be filled with something real, or you just find another way to fill it with something cheap.

- Sleep by 11 pm, up by 6 am. Every day.
- 1 hour of workout or movement. Every day.
- 1 hour of reading. Every day.
- 1.5 hours of DSA. Every day.
- Journaling. Every day.

None of these are new ideas. Every self-improvement list has them. The difference is I'm not doing them when I feel like it — they're non-negotiable, tracked daily, and the streak system holds me accountable even on days I don't want to.

Which brings us to the experiment itself.

## Part 3 — This Is Not a Detox. This Is an Experiment.

If you search "dopamine detox" right now, you'll find thousands of posts. Thirty-day challenges. Productivity influencers telling you to put your phone in a drawer for a weekend. People documenting how refreshed they felt after 24 hours offline. Good for them.

That's not what this is.

Everyone doing a dopamine detox has the same goal — *feel better*. Reset. Recharge. Come back to the apps a little calmer, a little more present. And then, quietly, come back to the apps.

I don't want to feel better and come back. I want to find out what I'm actually capable of.

There's a concept called neuroplasticity — the brain's ability to rewire itself based on what you repeatedly do and expose it to. It's thrown around a lot in self-help content, usually as a vague motivational idea. But it's real, and it cuts both ways. Every hour I spent toggling between reels and games was training my brain to need that — to crave the next hit, to find anything slower or harder completely unbearable. My attention span didn't shrink by accident. I built it that way, one scroll at a time.

So the question I actually want to answer is: **if I rewired it in the wrong direction, can I rewire it back?**

And more than that — *how far back?* Or further than back? What does my brain look like when it isn't running on fumes and cheap dopamine all day? I genuinely don't know. And I think that's the most interesting part.

This is why I set up two tracking systems to run alongside this experiment.

### Sumukh 2.0 — The Streak System

The first is a Google Sheet I'm calling Sumukh 2.0. Every day gets a row. Every habit — phone blocks, no Instagram, no games, no music, sleep by 11, up by 6, workout, journaling, diet, reading, DSA — gets a column. Green means I did it. Dark red means I didn't.

I started it on Day 1 knowing every cell would be red. That was intentional. Because the point isn't to start when you're ready — it's to start and let the data be honest about where you actually are.

### Dopamine Detox Log — The Brain Data

The second sheet is the one I'm more excited about. This is where I track what's actually happening to my brain over time — focus levels, mood, urge to relapse, output, and something I'm calling "phantom music," which is when your brain starts playing songs on its own because it's so conditioned to having audio input constantly. I fully expect this to happen.

But the real data lives in the weekly tests. Every week I sit down and take the same set of tests:

- **Human Benchmark tests** — reaction time, visual memory, number memory, sequence memory, verbal memory, typing speed, aim trainer. Pure cognitive performance, tracked over time.
- **Mental wellbeing tests** — WHO-5 wellbeing index, GAD-7 anxiety scale, PHQ-9 depression scale, Athens Insomnia Scale, Brief Resilient Coping Scale, Perceived Stress Scale.

I took all of these on Day 1 as my baseline. The numbers are in Part 4.

The goal is simple: nine months of clean data showing what happens to a brain — *my* brain — when you remove the noise and replace it with silence, structure, and actual hard work. No coached answers, no curated highlight reel. Just the numbers, week by week, honest.

If it works, the January post writes itself.
If it doesn't — that's a story too. Maybe a more important one.

## Part 4 — The Baseline: Where I Actually Am

This is the part I almost didn't write.

It's easy to talk about deleted apps and streak trackers and grand experiments. It's harder to show someone exactly where you're starting from — not the motivated, determined version of yourself, but the actual numbers. The honest picture.

But that's the whole point of doing this before the results are in. So here it is.

### Cognitive Performance — Human Benchmark

On Day 1, before anything had changed, I sat down and took every Human Benchmark test I'll be tracking weekly. These are the baselines everything gets measured against.

| Test | Day 1 Score |
|---|---|
| Chimp Test | 9 pts |
| Visual Memory | Level 8 |
| Number Memory | Level 10 |
| Sequence Memory | Level 6 |
| Verbal Memory | 20 words |
| Reaction Time | 205 ms |
| Typing Speed | 59 wpm |
| Aim Trainer | 564 ms |

Honestly, some of these aren't bad. Number memory at Level 10 surprised me. But reaction time at 205ms and sequence memory at Level 6 — those feel like a brain that's been running on autopilot for too long. Fast enough to scroll, not sharp enough to sustain.

We'll see what nine months does to these numbers.

### Mental Health — The Harder Numbers

This is the part I sat with for a while before deciding to include it.

I took six standardised mental health assessments on Day 0. I'm sharing them because pretending this experiment is purely about productivity or cognitive performance would be dishonest. The truth is that mental state and phone addiction aren't separate problems — they feed each other. The scrolling numbs something. The question is what.

| Test | My Score | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| WHO-5 Well-Being Index | 32 / 100 | Low wellbeing |
| GAD-7 Anxiety Scale | 19 / 21 | Severe anxiety |
| PHQ-9 Depression Scale | 22 / 27 | Severe range |
| Athens Insomnia Scale | 9 / 24 | Mild insomnia |
| Brief Resilient Coping Scale (BRCS) | 12 / 20 | Moderate resilience |
| Perceived Stress Scale | 26 / 40 | Moderate stress |

I'm not going to dress those up. The anxiety and depression scores are high. Severe, by the scale's own classification. I'm aware of that. I'm not in crisis — but I'm also not okay in the way someone who is actually okay is okay. There's a difference, and I've been using a phone to avoid feeling it.

I want to be clear about something: I'm not treating this experiment as a substitute for professional support. But I do believe that removing the things that are actively making this worse — the constant stimulation, the numbing, the avoidance — is a necessary step regardless of anything else. You can't heal something you're constantly anaesthetising.

### The Starting Line

So that's where I am on April 7, 2026. Day 0.

Anxiety through the roof. Low mood. Moderate stress. A brain that's been fed junk for long enough that it's forgotten what it feels like to be properly fed. And a streak sheet that's entirely red.

Not a great starting point. But it's an honest one. And honest is the only way this experiment means anything.

## Part 5 — What I'm Actually Hoping For

I want to be honest about this too, because it would be easy to end this post with some clean, confident statement about who I'm going to become by January. But that's not how I actually feel sitting here on Day 1.

What I feel is nervous.

Nervous that the silence is going to be louder than I expect. Nervous that without the constant noise I'll be left alone with thoughts I've been successfully avoiding for a long time. Nervous that I'll relapse — not once, but multiple times — and have to decide each time whether that means I've failed or just that this is hard. Nervous that I'll do everything right and the numbers won't move the way I hope.

But underneath all of that, there's something else.

I want to find out what I'm like when I'm not running from boredom. I want to know what my brain produces when it isn't constantly being fed someone else's content. I want to sit in a room with no music, no reels, no game to open, and find out what thoughts actually come up when there's nothing to drown them out. I want to read a book and finish it. I want to solve a hard DSA problem and feel something real about it — not the fake dopamine of a like or a streak notification, but the actual satisfaction of having done something difficult.

I want to feel like myself again. Whoever that is without all the noise.

And I want to make my dad proud. That one hasn't changed since the Monday evening this all started.

So that's the honest version. Not a highlight reel, not a transformation arc with a guaranteed ending. Just a person who decided that not trying was no longer an option — and documented it before knowing how it turns out.

The data will speak for itself in January.
